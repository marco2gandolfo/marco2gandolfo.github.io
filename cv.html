Academic profile

I have a comprehensive academic profile that strongly relates to cognitive psychology and neuroscience. My research thus far contributed to the functional understanding (the how) of the perceptual processes responsible of efficient perception of complex, meaningful visual stimuli (e.g. bodies, faces, objects and scenes). My ongoing and future research aims to combine psychophysics, correlational (fMRI), and causal (TMS) methods to test hypothesis surrounding overarching and fundamental questions in vision science: What is the goal of computations in visual cortex? (Marr, 1982); What is the nature of the representation in visual cortex, and how is this structured? Is the visual experience organized by knowledge, and therefore, to some extent, idiosyncratic to the observer?

Selected Publications

Causal evidence for expression of perceptual expectations in category-selective extrastriate regions.

Gandolfo M., Downing P.E. Current Biology, 29(15), 2496-2500, (2019), Cell Press.

Using fMRI-guided TMS, in two 2-sessions experiments we show that pre-target activity in visual cortex is causally necessary for the expression of perceptual expectations formed about an upcoming visual scene or person stimuli. This work brought the first compelling causal evidence that visual cortex activity related to the expectation of an upcoming, but not yet visible, stimulus (e.g. expecting to see a kitchen or a garden) is not epiphenomenal, but rather causally necessary for behavioural expectation effects (i.e. higher performance for the expected vs unexpected stimuli) to take place. This research epitomizes significant methodological advancement in the brain stimulation field by employing fMRI-guided TMS localization, and pre-registration of the procedures. The paper has been concurrently dispatched with a commentary from Dr. Press and Dr. Yon, experts on top-down effects on visuomotor processes.

Predictive processing of scene layout depends on naturalistic depth of field.

Gandolfo, M., Nägele, H., Peelen M.V. Psychological Science, 34(3), 394-405, (2023), Sage.

The human eye samples with snapshots the visual world, yet our perceptual experience is continuous. This is likely because our memories allow to fill-in the missing information between these snapshots. We show that the extent to which our memories influence visual scene processing (as measured by the boundary extension illusion, Intraub 1989) depends on how the image properties, particularly the depth of field, resemble day-to-day visual experiences. This work is transformative for the field, proving that it is imperative to use image sets that are ecologically representative when studying scene processing. Importantly, our image sets are available, and we contributed to enrich an existing boundary extension database (Bainbridge and Baker, 2020) with depth of field ratings on 1000 photographs.

Converging evidence that left extrastriate body area supports visual sensitivity to social interactions.

Gandolfo, M., Abassi, E., Balgova, E., Downing, P.E., Papeo, L., Koldewyn, K. (2024) Current Biology. Volume 34, Issue 2, 343 - 351.e5

The ability to efficiently perceive and understand others' social interactions is a fundamental human skill. Behavioural evidence points to an efficient perceptual process that treats interacting pairs of human figures (dyads) as a perceptual "primitive" that acts as a powerful signal, drawing and maintaining our attention to social interactions. Brain imaging studies provide correlational evidence that this phenomenon relies on the activity of focal visual brain regions that are involved in the perception of individual bodies. However, there is not yet any direct and causal evidence connecting the activity of these visual regions to the behavioural outcomes that express human sensitivity to visually observed social interactions.

In this article we solve this problem with a multi-method approach. With fMRI we demonstrate that the extrastriate body area (EBA), particularly in the left hemisphere, responds with high selectivity to visual stimuli of facing human dyads. We then confirm the unique nature of facing dyads with a task showing that facing dyads are disproportionally difficult to categorise following inversion (compared to non-facing dyads). Finally, we close the causal loop with fMRI-guided transcranial magnetic stimulation, showing that the normal activity of the left EBA causally contributes to behavioural visual sensitivity to social interactions.

These findings add to our understanding of human social perception, and of the brain regions that support such skills. Further, the multi-method approach provides significant insights into how to bridge the gap between brain research and behavioural observations, and expresses the core of my research approach – that is to provide rigorous causal evidence that visual cortex activity is influenced by top-down knowledge.
